Slide 8

In the early '90s at Sun Microsystems,
someone wrote down a list of false assumptions about distributed systems —
the things all of us quietly assume while writing a call to another service.
There are eight. I've put three on the slide —
the three you'll see in the code today.
The rest are worth a read — just google fallacies of distributed computing.

The first false assumption is The network is reliable.
	It isn't. Packets get lost, connections drop.

The second one Latency is zero.
	You've just seen what that one is worth —
	especially when someone's downloading something over the Wi-Fi in the background.

The third one  A remote call is the same as a local one.
	The most expensive fallacy of them all.
	A local method either works or throws up an exception — either way, you know straight away.
	A remote one has a third state: it stays silent, and nobody tells you for how long.

There are three answers to these three fallacies.
Retry: the network blinked, so try again.
Timeout: you don't know whether it will answer, so don't wait for it forever.
Circuit breaker: it's down, so cut it off and give it room to breathe.


Slide 9: Slow is worse than broken

I said the last fallacy is the most expensive one.
Why?
A slow call hurts because the number of things in progress at the same time grows with the response time.

Let's say we get a hundred new requests per second and each one takes fifty milliseconds.
So at any given moment, about five calls are in progress.

Now a second scenario. Same traffic, still a hundred requests per second,
but now each request takes six hundred milliseconds.
That means at any given moment, sixty of them are in progress.

So why would sixty at once be a problem?
Let's start on their side — the planner. It used to have five requests open. Now it has sixty.
Think about that for a second: it slowed down because it had too much work — and we've just handed it twelve times more.

And on our side? Where's the problem?
Threads? Could be — but mine are virtual, so they're cheap.
Database connections? Could be — but I return the connection to the pool before I call the planner.

So where is the problem?

The client booked a docking bay. 
So there's a row in the database holding that bay for that time slot — status HOLD.
Before we can confirm it, we need a route from the planner.
If the call succeeds — fine, the bay was going to be taken anyway. 
But if it fails, after two attempts, I've been blocking that bay for a long time. For nothing.

(bottom table)
So let me show you the first tool I put in place, and the simplest one: a timeout.
In my demo the timeout is eight hundred milliseconds 
— that's the longest I'm willing to wait for the planner.
Now, look at what it does with these calls.

Three hundred? It lets it through.
Nine hundred? It cuts it off. I get an error — and I know about it.
Six hundred? It lets it through, and as far as the timeout is concerned, that's a perfectly good success.

Remember: a timeout answers one question, and one only:
'Did it make it in time or not?'
So | what do I need | to spot a failure | that DOES make it in time?


Slide 10:

That red screen was a circuit breaker.
It works just like the fuse box at home. It isn't there to cut the power for good. It's there to stop the house from burning down. Our circuit breaker has three states.

CLOSED, OPEN and HALF-OPEN

CLOSED: traffic flows, and the breaker counts the whole time.
	It looks at the last ten calls.
	That's its window, and it moves forward with every new call.
	What exactly does it count? I'll get to that in a moment.

OPEN: it cuts the traffic off and gives the client a fallback, a plan B.
	In my case it's an honest no: a five-oh-three, with a message — come back in ten seconds.
	Taking the traffic off protects the other service — and it's usually the only way to let it recover.

HALF-OPEN: after ten seconds open, the breaker gives it another go.
	It lets three calls through and judges them by the same thresholds.
	Good and fast? Back to green.
	Bad or slow? It cuts off again for another ten seconds.

And now the answer to the puzzle I left you with: how many errors were on the counter?
Zero on the planner's side.
And yet the screen was red.
How was that possible?

Because my breaker has one window, but two counters.
When a call finishes, it gets two separate labels.
The first: how did it end — error or success?
The second: how long did it take? Anything over half a second counts as slow.

Let's take the same three calls as before.
Three hundred milliseconds, two hundred OK: nothing goes on either counter.
Nine hundred: the timeout cuts it off at eight hundred.
	That's an error, but it also took more than half a second, so it goes into both counters.
And six hundred? The timeout says success. The slow counter says plus one.

In the demo, every single call looked like that middle row: six hundred.
Error counter showed zero per cent.
Slow counter: a hundred per cent.
And it only takes one of the two to cross the threshold 
— fifty per cent in my case — to open the breaker.



Slide 11:

I promised you three answers. We've done the timeout. We've done the circuit breaker.
Now the third one: retry.

There's one rule for retry: only retry what could have worked in the first place.
Error five-hundred — the server got it wrong — or a timeout can be something temporary. 
The network blinked. A second attempt might just land on a healthy node.

Error four-hundred means the caller got it wrong — and that's deterministic. 
Your request was bad, and it'll be just as bad next time.

There are two exceptions, and anyone who calls a paid API knows them well:
429 'too many requests' and 408 'you took too long to send the request'.
Those are temporary, so you can retry them.

For all the other four-hundreds, if you ask the same thing again, you'll get the same answer.
As the internet says Einstein said: insanity is doing the same thing over and over and expecting different results.

What is worse every one of those attempts lands in the breaker as a failure.
You're feeding the breaker your own bugs.

Say you ignore the rejections, but count every other error.
A window of ten calls.
Five NPEs in a row, and the fifty per cent threshold is crossed.
What happens?

The breaker opens. On a service that's in perfect health.
The client gets a 503, 'come back later', for a bug that will never fix itself.
An alert goes off.
And whoever's on call, at three in the morning, gets up and checks the wrong service.


Slide 12:

The obvious fix? Ignore more. Ignore all of RuntimeException.
	Who's buying that? (a few hands)

	If you say yes, you've just blinded the breaker.

You've just ignored the parent of almost every exception in Java.

In the Resilience4j config, exceptions aren't matched by name.
They're matched by type, together with every type that inherits from it.
And the 'planner isn't responding' exception
		inherits from RuntimeException too.


🟡 What we've been doing here is a deny-list: a list of what not to count.

You can already see the problem.
If you ignore too few errors, the breaker cuts traffic off too early.
If you ignore too many, the breaker never cuts traffic off at all. You've blinded it.

There's no good middle ground, because the list of your own bugs never ends.

The answer is an allow-list. 
Instead of saying what to ignore, you say what to count.

You name what counts as the dependency failing. 
Everything else, by definition, isn't a failure.

And that allow-list has an end.



Slide 13:

In my code, the method with the circuit breaker also has a retry on it.

Now, these two want opposite things.
Retry says: don't give up, try again.
The breaker says: stop, you're making things worse.
Each one on its own is simple.
It only gets interesting when they sit on the same method, because one of them has to go first.

In Java, you add these mechanisms to a method as annotations: @Retry and @CircuitBreaker.
For each annotation, Resilience4j — that's the library I'm using 
— wraps the method in one layer.
That layer is called an aspect.

By default, retry is on the outside and the breaker is on the inside.
So what does that actually mean?
Retry doesn't know the breaker exists. It just calls a second time.
And the breaker doesn't know it's a retry. It just sees another call, and counts it.

🟡 Let's do an example with round numbers — not my real config.
The threshold is fifty per cent. Ten operations come in, two of them fail, and retry gets three attempts per failure.
How many failures does the breaker see? 
Twenty per cent?
That's how a person counts: by operations.
Twenty is less than fifty, so the breaker stays asleep, right?


Not necessarily. It stays asleep but,
the breaker doesn't count operations. It counts attempts.

Two failed operations, three attempts each: that's six failures.
Eight operations succeeded on the first try: that's eight successes.

The sum is fourteen calls, six of them red. Forty-three per cent.

Just two more failed attempts, and the breaker opens.

That isn't a bug in the library. It's the default configuration.

Because it counts attempts, the breaker reacts faster to a hard failure.


Slide 14 — To count or not to count

Now the real thing — code from my demo.
 Top box: the resilience config from Starport's YAML.

Look how little there is.
Three entries, three verbs: record, ignore, retry. That's the whole config.
Each one comes with a list of exception classes.
Let's read it top to bottom.

The first line, circuitbreaker.instances.trade-route-planner — that's the name I gave my circuit breaker.
Under it, two lists.
	Record exceptions: these count as the planner failing — too many of them, and the breaker opens.
	Ignore exceptions: here the planner is working fine, it just said no to our request — so that's not a failure, and it doesn't count at all.
Below that, the retry — same name.
One list: after these, we send the same request once more.


Now look at the whole thing and tell me: what repeats here? (someone: PlannerUnavailableException)
The class PlannerUnavailableException is in the first list and in the third.
That's not an accident.
Record asks: is this the dependency failing?
Retry asks: is it worth another go?
In practice that's one question:
if the planner is down, it's the planner's failure AND it's worth another try.
If your two answers don't match, you either have a very good reason, or a bug.

Now the real question: where's NullPointerException in all this? 
 Look at the bottom box — the moment the planner's answer comes back.
 That's where a planner error gets caught and given a name:
 a four hundred becomes rejected, a five hundred becomes unavailable. 
 
This code only names the planner's errors.
Our own bugs get no name.
So what does the breaker do with a nameless exception?
It checks the lists in the order of the three coloured boxes:
should I ignore it?
Should I record it?
And if it's neither — success.
Fair enough: the breaker isn't there to review your code.

The breaker answers one question: is the planner healthy?
So it should only watch my conversation with the planner. Nothing else.

But my old method did more than talk to the planner.
It also checked the input and mapped the response — my code, my bugs.
And the breaker, as we just saw, counts a nameless bug as a success.
A success… in the planner's health score. 
My bugs were polluting the planner's score.

The fix it's a smaller method.

The method annotated with Retry and Circuit breakeer should do one thing: the network call.
Checking and mapping moved out, to where the breaker isn't counting.
Now the only thing the breaker can see… is the planner. My bug stays my bug.

⚓ Configuration enforces the decision. The code makes it.

Slide 16 — Micrometer as a facade

Where did all those counters on the dashboard come from,
if nobody had written them? 
The answer is Micrometer.
It does for metrics — and for traces — what SLF4J does for logs:
your code talks to one API to create metrics, 
and where the data goes is specified in one dependency. 
I chose Prometheus.
Prometheus is the database: every fifteen seconds it asks the application for its counters, and stores them.
Grafana just draws them.

▎ 🟡 - left card
This is all it costs to get in.
Four dependencies — six if you also send traces.

Actuator — it exposes the state of the application under /actuator.
The Prometheus registry — the format Prometheus knows how to read.

Now the amber ones.
The Spring Cloud starter for Resilience4j — the annotations and the YAML binding.
AOP — the thing that actually reads those annotations.

The two small ones are for tracing — they send spans.
A span is a stopwatch with a label: one small operation, its name, how long it took — for example: route planning, 600 milliseconds.
A trace is all the spans from one request, glued together across every service it passed through.


▎ 🟢 - right card,
And this is everything we wrote ourselves to get metrics. All of it.
An Observation is Micrometer's way of saying: measure this block of code.
You give it a name — reservations.route.plan — and wrap the call in a lambda. That's it.

From that one name, you get two things at once:
a timer on the dashboard — how long, how often, how many failed —
and a span in the trace.

🟡 this Observation lives in the adapter — one level above the method
that carries @Retry and @CircuitBreaker.

▎ ⚓ Notice how simple that was.
